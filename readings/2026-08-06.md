# Big Questions, After School: Inside the New Wave of Teen Philosophy Clubs
*Reading time: ~7 minutes · Level: Bagrut (Band III)*

Every Tuesday afternoon, a group of high school students walks across the school **pavement**, backpacks heavy, and heads not to soccer practice or a music lesson, but to a small classroom where the only equipment needed is a chair and an open mind. Welcome to the modern philosophy club, a growing trend among teenagers who want to talk about life's biggest questions before they even finish their homework.

## A Club Built on Big Questions

The idea is simple: once a week, students sit in a circle and discuss one hard question. Do we really have **free will**, or is every choice we make already decided by our past? Is it fair for a small group of people to lead a whole country, the way ancient thinkers imagined a perfect **republic** would work? There are no wrong answers here, only better and worse arguments. A teacher or student volunteer usually opens the meeting by saying "**go ahead**" to whoever wants to speak first, and from there, the conversation is almost entirely student-led.

Club founder Noa Peretz, a twelfth grader, says the sessions grew out of a simple frustration. "In most classes, we learn facts," she explains. "But nobody ever asked us what we actually believe, or why." Her club now meets in schools across the country, and interest keeps growing. What started as a handful of curious students meeting in an empty classroom has turned into a small movement, with some clubs even organizing joint debates between different schools.

## From Ancient Greece to the School Hallway

Many of these clubs borrow ideas from classic texts, including Plato's *Republic*, a book written more than two thousand years ago that still asks questions people argue about today: What makes a fair society? Who should be in charge, and why? Students don't need to read the entire book. Instead, they often start with a short passage and try to **interpret** it together, working out what the author meant and whether they agree.

This process is rarely **smooth**. Members admit that early meetings can feel awkward, with long silences or students talking over one another. But most clubs settle into a rhythm after a few weeks, once members learn to listen as carefully as they speak. Some clubs even set a simple rule: before disagreeing with someone, you must first repeat their argument in your own words, just to prove you actually understood it.

## How These Clubs Actually Work

Running a philosophy club doesn't require much money or equipment, which makes it an especially **economical** activity for schools with tight budgets. All a group really needs is a room, some chairs, and, **at least** occasionally, a printed page with a quote or a short story to discuss. Some clubs invite a local university student to help **operate** the sessions and keep the conversation moving, though most are run entirely by teenagers themselves.

Studies on youth debate and discussion programs suggest that students who take part often become more confident public speakers and better listeners, **among other things**. Teachers who supervise these clubs also report that students bring the same careful thinking into other subjects, from history essays to science projects.

## Why Teens Keep Coming Back

For many members, the appeal isn't just the philosophy itself, but the chance to be taken seriously. In a philosophy club, a fifteen-year-old's opinion about justice or free will carries as much weight as anyone else's, as long as it's backed by a good argument. That sense of respect, students say, is rare and valuable.

As more schools start their own clubs, one thing seems clear: teenagers are hungry for a space to think out loud about the questions that matter most, and they don't need a fancy classroom to do it. Sometimes all it takes is a circle of chairs, a hard question, and someone willing to say "**go ahead**."
