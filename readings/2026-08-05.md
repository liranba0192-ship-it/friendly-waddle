# From Garage to Gallery: The Teen Sculptors Turning Junk into Art

*Reading time: ~7 minutes · Level: Bagrut (Band III)*

Every weekend, seventeen-year-old Noa Peretz walks into her family's **garage** and turns it into an art studio. Old bicycle wheels, broken chairs, and rusty pipes cover the floor. By the time she is finished, these forgotten objects will become a giant **sculpture** — a bird, a dragon, or sometimes a shape nobody can name. Noa is one of a growing number of teenagers across the country who are turning waste into wonder, one weekend at a time.

## A Hobby That Started Indoors

Noa's story began during a rainy winter, when she was stuck **indoors** with nothing to do. She found an old toolbox in the garage and started building small figures out of scrap metal, just to pass the time. What began as a boring afternoon soon became a passion she could not stop. "I just wanted to **keep on doing** it," she says. "Every model taught me something new about balance, weight, and shape." Within a year, her small figures had grown into sculptures taller than she is.

## On Your Own, or With a Team

Some young sculptors prefer to work **(all) on your own**, spending hours alone with their hands and their imagination. Noa is different. She likes to **participate** in group projects, working with **others** from her school's art club. Together, they collect materials, share tools, and give each other feedback. "When you build something this big," she explains, "it helps to have more than one pair of hands — and more than one pair of eyes."

This teamwork has led to real results. Last spring, the club built a twelve-foot whale out of plastic bottles collected from local beaches. The whale now hangs **above** the entrance of the town library, where visitors stop to take photos every day.

## When Art Is Meant to Frighten You

Not every sculpture is meant to be beautiful. For last year's autumn art festival, Noa's team built a sculpture designed to **frighten** visitors on purpose: a twisted metal creature with glowing red eyes, built entirely from old car parts. Crowds gathered around it every evening, some laughing nervously, some backing away in surprise. "Scary art is still art," Noa says with a smile. "It makes people feel something, and that is the whole point."

## A Movement Spreading Across the Region

What started as one teenager's rainy-day hobby has grown into something bigger. Art teachers across the **region** have noticed the trend and started similar clubs in their own schools. Several towns now hold small festivals where young sculptors display their work side by side, comparing ideas and techniques. Studies suggest that hands-on art projects like these can improve problem-solving skills and teamwork, which is one reason so many schools are eager to support them.

For Noa, the best part is not the finished sculpture — it is the process of turning something useless into something people want to look at. "Anyone can throw an old wheel in the trash," she says. "It takes a little imagination to turn it into a dragon."

Her advice for anyone who wants to try it is simple: start small, be patient, and do not be afraid if your first project does not look like much. As Noa puts it, "The garage is a great place to fail — and an even better place to succeed."
