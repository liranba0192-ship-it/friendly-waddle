# Hunting the "Frankenfish": Teens Join the Fight to Defeat an Invasion

*Reading time: ~7 minutes · Level: Bagrut (Band III)*

Picture a fish that can breathe air, survive out of water for days, and wriggle across wet grass to reach a new pond. It sounds like something from a horror movie, but it is real. American news outlets nicknamed this creature the "Frankenfish," and for the past twenty years, scientists, volunteers, and now even high school students have been trying to **get rid of** it before it spreads any further.

## A Monster in the Water

The northern snakehead is not from North America at all. It originally lived in rivers across East Asia, and it likely arrived in the United States through the aquarium and live-fish food trade. Someone probably released a few fish into a local pond, not realizing what would happen next. The snakehead has almost no natural predators in its new home, sharp teeth, and a huge appetite for smaller fish, frogs, and even ducklings. Within a few years, a handful of "escaped" fish became thousands, spreading through connected streams, canals, and lakes across several states.

What makes the snakehead especially hard to control is its unusual ability to survive outside water. Most fish die within minutes once they are removed from a river, but a snakehead can absorb oxygen directly from the air using a special organ near its gills. On damp ground, it can wriggle forward using its fins almost like tiny legs, which is exactly how the "Frankenfish" nickname began. Biologists warn that if this **monster** of a fish is not controlled, it could seriously damage the balance of local rivers and lakes, pushing out native species that took thousands of years to find their own place in the ecosystem.

## Teens on the Riverbank

To fight back, wildlife agencies in several states started community removal programs, and some now welcome student volunteers. On a typical Saturday, a group of teenagers gathers along the muddy **bank** of a river, nets and buckets in hand, while a biologist explains how to tell a snakehead apart from harmless native fish. Some volunteers use traditional rods; a few even try **fly** fishing, casting a light lure across the water while real flies buzz above the surface in the summer heat. Because snakeheads have sharp teeth and rough scales, volunteers are told to wear long **sleeves** and gloves before they reach into a bucket to remove one. It is messy, sometimes smelly work, but many students say it feels like real science, not just a school project.

## A Fight That Will Not Quit

Progress has been slow. Despite years of trapping, netting, and public awareness campaigns, nobody expects to **defeat** the snakehead completely any time soon. Still, the volunteers refuse to **quit**. Some towns now hold an annual "Snakehead Derby," a friendly fishing contest where anyone who catches one of the invasive fish can enter it to win a prize. At the closing ceremony last year, a young marine biology student gave a short **speech**, telling the crowd that every fish removed from the river is one less predator eating the eggs of native species. Her **speech** ended with a simple message: small actions, repeated often enough, truly add up.

## Small Steps, Big Hope

Recent studies suggest something encouraging: in a few closely monitored rivers, the snakehead population has shown a **slight** decline over the past two years, likely thanks to steady removal efforts. Scientists are careful not to celebrate too early, since fish numbers can rise again **shortly** after any drop, especially if just one breeding pair is left behind in a quiet corner of a lake. Even so, researchers hope that with continued help from citizen volunteers, including curious teenagers who show up on weekend mornings, rivers can gradually return closer to their natural balance.

For the students involved, the project offers something a regular classroom cannot: a direct, hands-on connection between what they read in a biology textbook and what is actually happening outside their front door. Many say that after a single morning on the water, they start noticing invasive plants and animals everywhere, from the local park to their own backyard. The Frankenfish may never fully disappear, but every net, every rod, and every long-sleeved volunteer standing on a muddy bank makes the invasion a little smaller than it was before.
